# Abstract reading checklist and base rates

Framework anchors: Friedman, *Fundamentals of Clinical Trials*; Guyatt, *Users' Guides
to the Medical Literature*; Greenhalgh, *How to Read a Paper*. Base rates: BIO/Informa/
QLS *Clinical Development Success Rates*; Wong, Siah and Lo, *Estimation of clinical
trial success rates and related parameters*, Biostatistics 20(2) 2019 (in the project KB).

## Read in this order — design before numbers

1. **Population.** Who was enrolled, and how does it differ from the label population
   or from the competitor's trial population? Enrichment is the most common reason a
   cross-trial comparison is meaningless.
2. **Comparator.** Placebo, standard of care, or an active comparator chosen to be
   beatable? An underpowered or outdated comparator arm is a red flag that survives
   into the label negotiation.
3. **Primary endpoint, and whether it is what is being reported.** If the abstract
   headlines a secondary or exploratory endpoint, ask why. This is the single highest-
   yield question in abstract reading.
4. **Statistical plan.** Alpha allocation, hierarchical testing, interim analyses and
   their spending function. A nominally significant p-value outside the testing
   hierarchy is not a positive trial.
5. **Data cut-off and maturity.** Event-driven endpoints at an early cut-off flatter
   the result. Compare events observed to events required.
6. **Effect size versus the clinically meaningful threshold**, not versus zero. A
   statistically significant, clinically trivial difference does not change practice
   and will not change reimbursement.
7. **Safety.** Discontinuation rate is usually more informative than the adverse-event
   table, because it aggregates tolerability into a single behavioural measure.
8. **Missing data and censoring.** Informative censoring is where survival curves are
   made.

## Base rates to anchor on

Published phase-transition benchmarks for the 2011–2020 period put overall likelihood of
approval from Phase I at roughly 8%, with the Phase II to Phase III transition around
29% — the weakest link in development. Modality and therapeutic area vary this
substantially: rare disease and biologics run above average, oncology Phase II to III
below.

Use these as the prior. The abstract adjusts the prior; it does not replace it. When an
analyst's post-abstract probability is far from the base rate, the burden is on the
adjustment to justify itself.

## Conference-specific mechanics worth knowing

- Title release generally precedes abstract release by weeks; abstract release precedes
  presentation. Each is a separate, dated information event.
- Late-breaking abstracts are typically released at or near presentation time.
- Presentation slot is assigned by the scientific committee and correlates with
  perceived importance — an oral slot for a small trial is a signal in itself.
- Some sponsors publish a press release at the same moment as the abstract, and the
  release wording versus the abstract data is itself informative when they diverge.
